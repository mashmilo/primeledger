Index funds are one of the most popular ways for everyday investors to build long-term wealth, and for good reason: they are simple, cheap and diversified. Instead of trying to pick winning shares, an index fund aims to match the performance of a market, such as the largest companies in a country or a broad slice of the global stock market.

That simplicity is the appeal, but index funds are not risk-free, and they are not a shortcut to quick gains. This guide explains how index funds work, what they cost, how to buy one in the US, UK, Canada and Australia, and the mistakes beginners commonly make. It is general education, not personal financial advice. Investments can fall as well as rise, you can lose money, and past performance does not predict future results. Rules, products and tax treatment vary by country and change over time, so check current details with your provider and a qualified adviser if needed.

## What is an index fund?

A market index is a list of securities that represents part of the market. Well-known examples include the S&P 500 in the US, the FTSE 100 in the UK, the S&P/TSX Composite in Canada and the S&P/ASX 200 in Australia. There are also global indexes that cover companies from many countries.

An index fund holds the securities in a chosen index, in roughly the same proportions, so its returns follow that index before costs. Because the fund simply tracks a list rather than relying on a manager's stock picks, it is called **passive** investing. Its opposite, an **actively managed** fund, tries to beat the market and usually charges more for the attempt.

### Index funds vs ETFs

You will often see "index fund" and "ETF" mentioned together.

| Feature | Traditional index (mutual) fund | Index ETF (exchange-traded fund) |
|---|---|---|
| How you buy | Directly from the fund provider or via a platform | On a stock exchange through a brokerage, like a share |
| Pricing | Usually once per day | Continuously during market hours |
| Minimum investment | Sometimes a set minimum | Often the price of one unit, with fractional options at some brokers |
| Availability | Common in the US, UK and Australia | Widely available in all four countries |
| Costs | Ongoing fund fee | Ongoing fund fee plus possible brokerage and spread costs |

Both can track the same index. The right choice usually comes down to what your platform offers and how the costs add up for the amount you plan to invest. Note that product structures vary by country, so some of these details may differ where you live.

## Why people choose index funds

- **Diversification:** One fund can hold hundreds or thousands of companies, reducing the impact of any single company failing.
- **Low costs:** Passive funds generally charge lower ongoing fees than actively managed funds.
- **Simplicity:** There is no need to research individual companies.
- **Transparency:** The holdings follow a published index, so you can see what you own.
- **Discipline:** A fixed approach can help you avoid emotional trading decisions.

Diversification reduces certain risks, but it does not remove market risk. If the whole market falls, an index fund tracking it will usually fall too.

## The risks you need to understand

Being honest about risk is part of investing well.

- **Market risk:** Share prices can fall sharply, sometimes for long periods. You should be prepared for the value of your investment to drop.
- **Concentration risk:** Some indexes are dominated by a small number of large companies or one sector, so a "broad" index may be less diversified than it looks.
- **Currency risk:** If a fund holds assets in other currencies, exchange rate moves can raise or lower your returns.
- **Tracking difference:** The fund may not match its index exactly because of fees and how it is managed.
- **Behaviour risk:** Selling in a panic after a fall can lock in losses. Many investors damage their own results by reacting to short-term news.

Money you may need within a few years generally does not belong in stock-based funds. Savings accounts or other low-risk options may suit short-term goals better. See our guide to [high-yield savings accounts](/high-yield-savings-accounts/) for that side of the picture.

## Understanding the costs

Costs compound against you just as returns compound for you, so they deserve attention.

### Ongoing fund fee

Each fund charges an annual fee, shown as an **expense ratio** in the US and Canada (the Canadian version is often called the management expense ratio, or MER), as **ongoing charges figure (OCF)** in the UK, and as **management costs** or similar in Australia. It is taken from the fund's assets, so you do not see a separate bill.

### Platform and trading costs

- Brokerage or platform fees, which may be a flat fee, a percentage, or free for some products
- Commission on each trade at some brokers
- The bid-ask spread on ETFs
- Currency conversion fees if you buy funds priced in another currency

### A simple worked example

The figures below are hypothetical and only illustrate how fees work, not what any real fund will return.

Imagine you invest 10,000 and the investment grows at the same 6% per year before fees in both cases, over 20 years.

| Annual fee | Approximate value after 20 years |
|---|---|
| 0.10% | Around 31,000 |
| 1.00% | Around 26,000 |

Over two decades, a seemingly small fee difference can leave you several thousand better or worse off. Real returns will vary and may be negative in some years, so treat this only as a way to see why costs matter.

## How to choose an index fund

### Decide what you are trying to do

Define your goal and time horizon first. Retirement in 25 years is a very different plan from buying a house in 4 years.

### Pick the index

Common choices include:

- **Broad domestic market index:** exposure to your home country's companies
- **Global or world index:** companies from many countries, which spreads risk across economies
- **Bond index:** government or corporate bonds, usually lower volatility than shares but not risk-free
- **Sector or thematic index:** narrower and generally riskier, and not usually the best starting point

Many beginners hold a broad global equity fund, sometimes combined with a bond fund. That is a common approach, not a recommendation for you personally.

### Compare the details

When shortlisting funds, check:

1. **Fees:** the expense ratio, OCF or MER
2. **Index tracked:** and how diversified it really is
3. **Fund size and provider:** larger, established funds tend to be easier to buy and sell
4. **Distribution policy:** whether income is paid out to you (distributing) or reinvested automatically (accumulating), where both options exist
5. **Tracking difference:** how closely the fund has followed its index
6. **Domicile and tax treatment:** this can matter, especially for ETFs bought across borders

## Where to hold your investments: tax wrappers

The account you use can matter as much as the fund. The following is a summary only, and limits and rules change, so confirm current details with your tax authority.

| Country | Common account types | General idea |
|---|---|---|
| United States | 401(k), IRA, Roth IRA, taxable brokerage account | Retirement accounts offer tax advantages with rules on contributions and withdrawals |
| United Kingdom | Stocks and shares ISA, SIPP, general investment account | ISAs shelter growth and income from tax within annual limits; SIPPs are for retirement |
| Canada | TFSA, RRSP, non-registered account | TFSA growth is generally tax-free; RRSP contributions may be tax-deductible, with tax on withdrawal |
| Australia | Superannuation, standard investment account | Super has concessional tax treatment with access rules; investments outside super are taxed under normal rules |

If you can invest through a tax-advantaged account, it is worth understanding it before choosing where to place your fund. Also check whether your employer offers a retirement plan or contributions, as that can influence your decision. ## Step-by-step: how to buy your first index fund

1. **Build a safety net first.** Many people keep an emergency fund in cash and clear high-interest debt before investing. Your situation may differ.
2. **Set your goal and timeline.** Longer horizons can generally tolerate more share exposure than shorter ones.
3. **Choose an account type.** Consider tax-advantaged options available in your country.
4. **Pick a regulated broker or platform.** Check that it is authorised in your country and that your assets are protected under local investor protection rules. Compare fees, available funds and ease of use.
5. **Select your fund or funds.** Compare fees, index and structure as outlined above.
6. **Decide how to invest.** You can invest a lump sum, or spread money over time with regular contributions. Regular investing can reduce the impact of poor timing, but over long periods a lump sum has often done as well or better when markets rise. Neither approach guarantees a result.
7. **Automate and review.** Set up regular contributions if you can, and review your holdings about once a year rather than reacting to daily news.

## Common beginner mistakes

- **Chasing last year's best performer.** A fund that did well recently may not repeat it.
- **Paying high fees without a reason.** If two funds track the same index, the cheaper one usually has an advantage.
- **Over-trading.** Frequent buying and selling adds costs and often hurts results.
- **Putting everything in one narrow theme.** A single sector can fall hard.
- **Investing money you will soon need.** Short-term cash needs should be kept safe.
- **Ignoring tax rules.** Using the wrong account or missing allowances can reduce what you keep.
- **Panic selling.** Market falls are normal. Decide in advance how you would respond.

## FAQ

### Are index funds safe?

Index funds are diversified, but they are not safe in the sense of guaranteeing your money. Their value goes up and down with the market, and you can lose some or all of what you invest in a severe downturn. They are generally considered better suited to long-term goals than short-term needs.

### How much money do I need to start investing in index funds?

It depends on the provider. Some brokers allow you to start with small amounts or buy fractional units, while some mutual funds set minimums. Check fees too, because a flat platform fee can take a larger share of a small investment.

### Index fund or ETF: which is better?

Neither is better in every case. Both can track the same index at a low cost. Choose based on what your platform offers, how fees and trading costs compare for your situation, and how you want to invest, such as regular automatic contributions versus buying on an exchange.

### Do index funds beat actively managed funds?

Studies comparing the two often find that many active funds fail to beat their benchmark after fees over long periods, but results vary by market and time frame, and some active funds do outperform. The lower cost of index funds is one reason many investors choose them. No approach is guaranteed to win.

## Conclusion

Index funds offer a straightforward way to invest: broad diversification, low costs and little need for stock-picking skill. They still carry real market risk, so they suit money you can leave invested for the long term, and not money you may need soon.

Start by clarifying your goal and timeline, choose a suitable tax-advantaged account where one is available, compare funds on index, cost and structure, and invest consistently without reacting to every headline. If your situation is complex, a regulated financial adviser in your country can help tailor the approach.
