Mortgage rates are the single biggest driver of what a home loan costs you over time. A difference of even a fraction of a percentage point can add up to thousands over the life of a loan, which is why comparing properly is worth the effort. But the headline rate is only part of the story: fees, loan structure and your own finances all shape what you actually pay.

This guide explains how mortgage rates work in the US, UK, Canada and Australia, how to compare offers like for like, and what you can do to improve the deal you are offered. It is general information, not personal financial or mortgage advice. Rates, lending rules and products change often and vary by lender and country, and your home may be at risk if you do not keep up repayments. Check current details with lenders and consider speaking with a regulated broker or adviser.

## How a mortgage rate works

A mortgage is a loan secured against property. The interest rate is the annual cost of borrowing, expressed as a percentage of what you owe. Your repayments typically cover interest plus some repayment of the original amount borrowed (the principal), though some loans allow interest-only periods.

What you are offered depends on:

- **Your finances:** income, existing debts, savings and credit history
- **The deposit or equity:** the loan-to-value ratio (LTV), meaning the loan as a share of the property's value
- **The loan type:** fixed, variable, term length and repayment structure
- **Market conditions:** lenders' own funding costs and the wider interest rate environment
- **The property:** type, location and sometimes whether it is your home or an investment

A larger deposit generally means a lower LTV, which many lenders reward with better rates because they see less risk.

## Fixed, variable and other rate types

| Rate type | How it works | Main advantage | Main risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fixed rate | Rate stays the same for a set period | Predictable repayments | May cost more to exit early; you miss out if rates fall |
| Variable or standard variable | Rate can rise or fall at the lender's discretion or with a benchmark | May fall when rates fall | Repayments can rise, sometimes sharply |
| Tracker (common in the UK) | Moves in line with a set benchmark plus a margin | Transparent link to the benchmark | Payments can rise as the benchmark rises |
| Split or hybrid | Part fixed, part variable | Balances certainty and flexibility | More complex; check terms for each portion |

### How this differs by country

- **United States:** The 30-year fixed-rate mortgage is the most common product, and fixed terms of 15 or 20 years also exist. Adjustable-rate mortgages (ARMs) are fixed for an initial period, then adjust.
- **United Kingdom:** Most borrowers choose a fixed rate for an initial period, commonly two or five years, after which the loan typically moves to the lender's standard variable rate unless you remortgage. Overall terms are often 25 to 35 years.
- **Canada:** Mortgages usually have a term, often five years, within a longer amortisation period such as 25 years. You renew or switch at the end of each term. Fixed and variable options are both common.
- **Australia:** Variable-rate loans are widely used, with fixed periods commonly offered for shorter terms, after which loans generally revert to a variable rate. Offset accounts and redraw facilities are common features.

## The rate is not the whole cost

Comparing only the interest rate can mislead you. Check these too:

- **Arrangement, application or settlement fees:** upfront charges that may be added to the loan or paid in cash
- **Valuation and legal fees:** some lenders cover these, others do not
- **Ongoing or package fees:** annual or monthly charges
- **Early repayment or break costs:** penalties for paying off or switching during a fixed period
- **Mortgage insurance:** often required in some markets when your deposit is small, such as private mortgage insurance in the US or mortgage default insurance in Canada, or lenders mortgage insurance in Australia
- **Cashback offers:** these can offset costs but may come with a higher rate or clawback conditions

Most countries have a standardised comparison measure, such as the APR in the US and UK, or the comparison rate in Australia, designed to include some fees. These are useful, but they rely on assumptions, so always compare the full cost over the period you expect to hold the loan.

## What lenders look at

Lenders assess whether you can afford the loan and whether you are likely to repay it.

### Affordability and income

They will review your income, regular spending, existing debts and dependants. Many lenders use stress tests, meaning they check you could still afford repayments at a higher rate than the one you are offered.

### Credit history

A strong record of on-time payments and low credit usage generally helps. Errors on your credit report can hurt you, so check your report with the relevant agencies before applying and correct mistakes.

### Deposit and LTV

A bigger deposit usually widens your choices and may unlock lower rates. A smaller deposit can still work, but may mean higher rates or extra insurance costs.

### Employment type

Employed borrowers usually have a simpler application. Self-employed or contract workers are commonly asked for more evidence, such as several years of accounts or tax returns.

## How to compare mortgage offers: step by step

### Step 1: Know your budget

Work out what you can comfortably afford, including repayments, property taxes or council rates, insurance and maintenance. Do not rely only on the maximum a lender will offer.

### Step 2: Check and improve your credit profile

Review your credit reports, fix errors, avoid new credit applications shortly before applying, and keep existing accounts in good order.

### Step 3: Gather documents

Typically you will need:

1. Proof of identity and address
2. Recent payslips or business accounts and tax documents
3. Bank statements
4. Details of existing debts and commitments
5. Evidence of your deposit and its source

### Step 4: Get a pre-approval or agreement in principle

Many markets offer a preliminary assessment, known as pre-approval in the US, Canada and Australia, or an agreement in principle in the UK. It shows what a lender may be willing to offer and strengthens your position when making an offer on a property. It is generally not a final commitment, so conditions still apply.

### Step 5: Compare several lenders

Look at banks, credit unions or building societies, and online lenders. Consider using a broker, who can access many lenders, but ask how they are paid and whether they cover the whole market.

### Step 6: Compare like for like

Use the same loan amount, term and deposit for every quote, then compare rate, fees, features and total cost over the period you expect to keep the loan.

### Step 7: Read the terms before you commit

Look closely at early repayment rules, whether the loan can be moved to another property, what happens when a fixed period ends and what features are included.

## Ways to get a better rate

- **Increase your deposit** where possible, to lower your LTV
- **Improve your credit record** before applying
- **Reduce other debts,** which can improve affordability
- **Shop around and negotiate,** since lenders may match or improve offers
- **Consider a shorter term** if you can afford higher repayments, as total interest is usually lower
- **Review at the end of your fixed period.** In the UK, Canada and Australia, switching or renegotiating at renewal can save money, but check any fees first
- **Look at features that reduce interest,** such as offset accounts in Australia, but only if the rate and fees still make sense

## Common mistakes to avoid

- **Focusing only on the lowest rate.** High fees or restrictive terms can cancel the saving.
- **Borrowing the maximum.** Leave room in your budget for rate rises and unexpected costs.
- **Making large purchases or changing jobs mid-application.** This can affect approval.
- **Not checking early repayment charges.** They can be costly if you sell or refinance early.
- **Letting a fixed rate expire without acting.** You may move to a higher standard rate by default.
- **Giving inaccurate information.** Misstatements can lead to a declined application or serious consequences later.
- **Skipping professional help in complex cases.** Self-employment, adverse credit or unusual properties may benefit from specialist advice.

## FAQ

### Should I choose a fixed or variable mortgage rate?

It depends on your budget, risk tolerance and plans. A fixed rate gives certainty, which suits tight budgets. A variable rate may be cheaper if rates fall but can rise. Nobody can reliably predict rate movements, so choose the option you could still afford if rates went up.

### Does checking mortgage rates hurt my credit score?

Browsing rates or using an eligibility checker is often a soft inquiry that does not affect your score, but a full application usually involves a hard inquiry. Rules differ by country and lender, so ask before you apply. Multiple applications in a short time can have an impact.

### How much deposit do I need?

It varies by country, lender and loan type. Some lenders accept small deposits, but you may pay higher rates or extra insurance costs. A larger deposit usually improves your options. Check the minimums for your market and any government schemes you may be eligible for.

### When should I refinance or switch my mortgage?

Consider it when your fixed period or term is ending, when your finances have improved, or when a significantly better deal is available. Always weigh potential savings against switching costs, such as exit fees, legal costs and new arrangement fees.

## Conclusion

Getting a good mortgage is about the whole package, not just the headline rate. Understand the rate types in your country, set a realistic budget, strengthen your credit and deposit where you can, and compare offers on the same terms, including fees and early repayment rules.
